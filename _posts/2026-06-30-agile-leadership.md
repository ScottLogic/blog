Often, it seems, leadership and agility are discussed as separate concerns. In this post I'll discuss not only where and how they overlap but contend that they are in fact inseparably intertwined as concepts.

## Leadership and Agility

Some time back I gave a short talk on [Agile2](https://agile2.net/) to the Scott Logic Agile Community of Practice. It's a concept that I came across quite by accident, which bills itself as "the next iteration of agile". Agile2 seeks, in their own words:

> [make] a fresh start ... not an incremental one: a start that is informed by the first Agile experiment, but that does not hesitate to make major departures: a new beginning.

One of the things that struck me while researching the topic was the huge focus on leadership in Agile2; indeed, a whole section of the documentation is devoted to it. The authors claimed that the original manifesto "dismissed the importance of leadership" the first time round. By making leadership such a major part of their doctrine, they seemed to be implicitly including leadership as part of their definition of what it means to be agile.

I remember thinking at the time that I didn't wholly agree that leadership was necessarily "dismissed" as not important; rather, it felt like a separate topic to the concerns of the manifesto and its principles, which focus more on process and teams than on the nuances of leadership. This, however, raises the question of whether leadership is indeed a separate concern from agility. Leadership is always a factor, of course, in the success or failure of a team, but does it exist independently of the team's ability to be agile? We often talk of agility as something that, while of course augmented or facilitated by good leadership, is nonetheless independent of it. Can agility help to prop up or even be a substitute for poor leadership, or is it instead the case that poor leadership restricts how agile you can be? Likewise, can we substitute agility with good leadership? Is our ability to be agile less important if we have good leadership overall?

## Mission Command

In his book The Habit of Excellence, Lt Col. Langley Sharp (MBE) describes the British Army's approach to leadership, how it has evolved, and why it works. He draws parallels between the Army's changing approach to leadership in the years following the Second World War and agile software development in industry. Most importantly, he describes the introduction to the British Army of the Mission Command philosophy:

> ... this is an approach which empowers subordinate commanders and promotes initiative as well as freedom and speed of action. Critically, it focuses on achievement of higher intent through mission-type orders. It empowers leaders at every level and is intended to generate agility and tempo.

The goals of this approach — agility and tempo — are exactly the same goals that many software teams strive for, and, as in software, this approach has been hard won through experience and situational requirements.

In the years following the Second World War, the conflicts in which the British Army has been engaged have changed notably. Rather than fighting wars against nation-states, the British Army has increasingly been involved in non-standard engagements such as counter-insurgency and peace-keeping operations, examples of which include conflicts in Northern Ireland, Iraq and Afghanistan. One of the common themes of these engagements has been the tendency for situations on the ground to escalate rapidly and without warning, and often to just as quickly dissipate. This rapid change in the situation on the ground has meant it is rarely practical or even possible for communications to be sent back to senior commanders and for orders to be relayed quickly enough to keep up with changes. Ultimately, this means that junior leaders are often left to make critical leadership decisions as the situation evolves under their own initiative. This has led to a change in leadership style overall, moving away from commanders keeping tight control over the execution of a plan and towards an approach where commanders instead lay out the mission and the boundaries to operate within, leaving the implementation up to more junior leaders. This approach gives those junior leaders the flexibility and freedom to make decisions as they see fit within the confines of the parameters set out and in line with the mission, even contravening direct orders should they feel it necessary in the wider context of the mission.

Whilst the context is of course different, similar problems exist in software development. The requirement to facilitate quick decision-making in order to respond quickly and effectively to ever-evolving, and often ill-defined, situations has driven what we know today as agile development. Central to this approach is the need to empower leaders on the ground to make decisions based on the current situation without the need to involve senior management directly in each case and without fear of reprisal or reprimand for making autonomous decisions. Agile development is all about moving quickly and being flexible when building software, and in order for that to be effective we need to ensure our leaders and leadership style are well adapted to fit this approach. In the same way we struggle to be agile within rigid processes, we also struggle to be agile if the leaders at the top do not empower subordinates via delegation of command.

A crucial facet of this, in both military and civilian contexts, is trust. Leaders must trust their people — not blindly, but by setting out the intent and facilitating the mission, trusting their people to make decisions and ask questions as needed. It also requires followers to trust that their leaders are setting the right direction and that they will be given the freedom to make decisions and make mistakes without blame. Lastly, it requires that team members trust each other, at a peer-to-peer level, to get the job done and to work in the best interests of the team. This deep and multi-leveled application of trust can be challenging to build but is essential to a Mission Command style of leadership and, in turn, to an agile team.

No conversation on teams, trust and leadership is complete without considering communication. Communication is, as in many areas, essential to this approach ... a two-up, two-down approach, where a leader's intent should be communicated and understood at least two levels below and likewise two levels up the chain of command. For a team to function efficiently, it is just as important in software that senior stakeholders are aware of the direction and goals of a team at a high level as it is for the more junior developers to understand what the aims of a project or sprint are in a larger context than the particular piece of work they are currently developing.

My conclusion is that, while leadership may be, in an overall sense, a larger and even sometimes separate topic from what we would call agility in relation to software development, agility itself is closely intertwined with leadership. It is facilitated by it, dependent on it, and I would go as far as to say that it cannot exist without it. For any agile team to be successful or to consider itself agile, there must be effective leadership that enables that agility. Agility is more a mindset than it is a process. It is, in essence, a human endeavor and is about people and teams that comprise it rather than processes and tools. Teams of people need leadership, also a human endeavor, and leaders must facilitate an agile approach through trust and delegation of command, allowing teams to make decisions where required and trusting subordinate leaders to work in the best interests of the mission within the defined parameters.

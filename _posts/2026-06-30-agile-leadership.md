Often, it seems, leadership and agility are discussed as separate concerns. In this post I'll discuss not only where and how they overlap but contend that they are in fact inseparably intertwined as concepts.

Some time back I gave a short talk on [Agile2](https://agile2.net/) to the Scott Logics Agile Community of Practice. It's a concept that I came across, quite by accident which bills itself as "the next iteration of agile". Agile2 seeks to, in their own words:

> [make] a fresh start ... not an incremental one: a start that is informed by the first Agile experiment, but that does not hesitate to make major departures: a new beginning

One of the things that struck me while researching the topic was the huge focus on leadership in Agile2, indeed a whole section of the documentation is devoted to it. The authors claimed that the original manifesto "dismissed the importance of leadership" the first time round. By making leadership such a major part of their doctrine they seemed to be implicitly including leadership as part of their definition of what it means to be agile.

I remember thinking at the time that I didn't wholly agree that leadership was nessesraliy "dismissed" as not important, but rather it felt like a separate topic to the concerns of the manifesto and its principles, which focus more on process and teams over the nuances of leadership. This however raises the question of if leadership is indeed a separate concern to agility. Leadership is always a factor of course in the success or failure of a team, but does it exist independently of the teams ability to be agile? We often talk of agility as something that, while of course augmented or facilitated by good leadership, is nonetheless independent of it. Can agile help to prop up or even be a substitute for poor leadership or is it instead the case that poor leadership restricts how agile you can be. Likewise can we substitute agility with good leadership, is our ability to be agile less important if we have good leadership overall?

In his book The Habit of Excellence, Lt Col. Langley Sharp (MBE) describes the British Army's approach to leadership, how it has evolved and why it works. He draws parallels between the changing approach to leadership within the Army over the years following the second world war and agile software development in industry. Most importantly he describes the introduction to the British Army of Mission Command:

> ... This is an approach which empowers subordinate commanders and promotes initiative as well as freedom and speed of action. Critically, it focuses on achievement of higher intent through mission-type orders. It empowers leaders at every level and is intended to generate agility and tempo.

The goals of this approach, agility and tempo are exactly the same goals that many software teams strive for and as in software, this approach has been hard won through experience and the situational requirements of the past.

In the years following the second world war, the conflicts the British Army has been engaged in have changed notably. Rather than fighting wars against nation states, the British Army have increasingly been involved in non-standard engagements such as counter insurgency and peace keeping operations, examples of which are conflicts in Northern Ireland, Iraq and Afghanistan. One of the common themes of these engagements has been the tendency for situations on the ground to escalate rapidly and without warning and often to, just as quickly dissipate. This rapid change of the situation on the ground has meant it is rarely practical or even possible for reports to be sent back up to senior commanders and orders then relayed, ultimately meaning that junior leaders on the ground are often left to make critical leadership decisions as the situation evolves. This has lead to a change in leadership style, moving away from commanders keeping tight control over the execution of a plan and towards an approach where commanders instead layout the mission and the boundaries to operate within, leaving the implementation up to more junior leaders on the ground. This approach gives those junior leaders the flexibility and freedom to make decisions as they see fit within the confines of the parameters set out and in line with the mission, even contravening direct orders should they feel it nessesarry in the wider content of the mission.

Whilst the context is of course different, similar problems exist in software development. The requirement to facilitate quick decision making in order to respond quickly and effectively to ever evolving, and often  ill-defined situations, has driven what we know today as agile development. Central to this approach is the need to empower leaders on the ground to make decisions based on the current situation without the need to involve senior management directly in each case and without fear of reprisal. Agile development is all about how we can move quickly and be flexible when building software and in order for that to be effective we need to ensure our leaders and leadership style is well adapted to fit with this approach. In the same way we struggle to be agile within rigid processes, we also will struggle to be agile if the leaders at the top do not enable the flexibility of leadership which derives from delegation of command.

A crucial facet of this, in both military and civilian context is trust. Leaders must trust in their people, not trust blindly but rather setting out the intent and facilitating the mission, trusting in their people to make decisions and ask questions as needed. It also requires followers to trust that their leaders are setting the right direction and that they will be given the freedom to make decisions and mistakes without reprimand. Lastly it requires that team members trust each other, at a peer to peer level, to get the job done and to work in the best interests of the team. This deep and multi-leveled application of trust can be challenging to build but is essential to a Mission Command style of leadership and in turn to an agile team.

No conversation on teams, trust and leadership is complete without communication. Communication is, as in many areas, essential to this approach. ... a two up, two down approach, where a leaders intent should be communicated and understood at least two levels below and likewise also two levels up the chain of command. For a team to function efficiently it is just as important in software that senior stakeholders are aware of the direction and goals of a team at a high level as it is for the more junior developers to understand what the aims of a project or sprint are in a larger context than the particular piece of work they are currently developing. 

My conclusion then is that, while leadership may be, in an overall sense, divorced from what we would call agility in relation to software development, agility itself is closely intertwined with leadership, it is facilitated by it and in fact, cannot exist without it. For any agile team to be successful or to consider themselves to be agile, there must be effective leadership which enables this approach. Agility is more a mindset than it is a process. It is in essence, a human endeavor and is about people and teams the comprise rather than processes and tools. Teams of people need leadership, also a human endeavor, and leaders must facilitate an agile approach through trust and delegation of command, allowing teams to make decisions where required and trusting to subordinate leaders to work in the best interests of the mission, within the defined parameters.
